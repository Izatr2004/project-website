01 / Approach

Three exposures, one color image.

Prokudin-Gorskii photographed each scene through blue, green, and red filters. The scans contain three grayscale exposures stacked vertically, with blue at the top, green in the middle, and red at the bottom. Simply stacking them as RGB leaves colored outlines because the camera and scene could move between exposures.

I divide each scan into three equal-height channels and use blue as the reference. I convert the data to floating point between 0 and 1, dividing 8-bit images by 255 and 16-bit images by 65535. Then I find the horizontal and vertical shifts for green and red independently and combine the aligned channels in RGB order.

I use normalized cross-correlation (NCC) to compare the channels. For each candidate shift, I subtract the mean from each scoring region, divide by its L2 norm, and take their dot product. A larger score means a better match:

NCC(A, B) = Σ[(A − mean(A))(B − mean(B))]
/ [√Σ(A − mean(A))² · √Σ(B − mean(B))²]

Mean subtraction removes an overall brightness offset, and normalization reduces sensitivity to overall contrast. It still compares intensity patterns: objects with very different brightness across color filters can cause the wrong alignment.

The dark and light scan borders are not scene content. I exclude a fixed interior margin when scoring and keep the scoring window inside the valid overlap for every candidate shift. This prevents wrapped pixels from contributing to NCC. The same parameters are used for every image.

02 / Single-scale alignment

Search a small window.

For each of the three small JPEGs, I try every integer displacement from −15 to +15 pixels in both directions. The nested loops check 31 × 31 = 961 candidates per channel and keep the shift with the highest NCC. These images are small enough that this exhaustive search is fast.

Below, the cathedral comparison shows the colored outlines before alignment and the result after alignment. The three results that follow use the single-scale search.

03 / Multi-scale alignment

Start small, then refine.

The full-resolution TIFFs are roughly 3,000 × 4,000 pixels per channel, and their shifts can be much larger than 15 pixels. Enlarging the exhaustive search at full resolution would make it expensive to compare every candidate.

  1. Build a pyramid by repeatedly halving the channel dimensions with area-based resizing, which averages pixels when downsampling.
  2. At the smallest level, search the full ±15-pixel window with the same intensity-based NCC used above.
  3. Double the best displacement when moving to the next larger level, then search a small neighborhood around that estimate.
  4. Repeat until the original resolution, and apply the final shifts to the original full-size channels using NumPy rolls.

I stop downsampling when the largest channel dimension is at most 400 pixels. I use a ±15-pixel search at that coarsest level, a ±2-pixel refinement at every larger level, and a 15% scoring margin on every side. The TIFFs have five levels; the JPEGs are already smaller than 400 pixels, so their pyramid has one level and gives the same result as the single-scale search.

The previews remove a fixed 10% from each edge for presentation; the linked full-size outputs preserve the complete frame. This is a fixed display crop, not automatic border detection.

06 / Discussion

What worked, and what didn’t.

The three single-scale JPEG results align well. The pyramid results also align the main structures in 13 of the 14 supplied images and all three additional examples. Emir is the clear failure: NCC does not correctly align the red channel to blue, leaving visible colored outlines around the face and clothing. The blue robe is bright in one exposure and dark in another, so matching raw intensity patterns can favor an incorrect displacement. Subtracting a mean and normalizing contrast cannot account for those local differences. I kept this result visible because it shows the limitation of the required intensity-based metric.

Translation also cannot correct movement within the scene, differences in camera rotation, or damage on a plate. Small colored fringes around moving leaves, water, or people can remain even when the stationary structures align. The fixed preview crop removes most scan borders, but the full-size images retain them so the complete output can be inspected.

I kept this implementation to the required CS180 methods: single-scale NCC and a pyramid using NCC on raw channel intensities. I did not add edge features, automatic cropping, contrast adjustments, or white balancing.
